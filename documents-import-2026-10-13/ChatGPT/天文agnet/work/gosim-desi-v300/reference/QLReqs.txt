Requirements of the DESI QuickLook Pipeline (v1.0)

Robert Kehoe, Sami Kama, Govinda Dhungana (SMU), Adam Bolton, Kyle Dawson (Utah), Stephen Bailey, David Schlegel (LBL), Klaus Honscheid (OSU), Luiz da Costa, Angelo Fausti Neto, Ricardo Ogando (LIneA)

	The operation demands on the DESI spectrograph system require efficient scheduling of observation of exposures with optimal performance of the telescope at all times.  This requires that the spectrograph data be examined comprehensively on the mountain to provide metrics to establish this level of operation, and when necessary to provide experts with the information needed to assess and address observed performance deviations.  A ‘Quick Look’ spectrographic pipeline is envisioned to address these needs, and the requirements of this system are the subject of this paper.

1. QuickLook in the DESI Data System: 
The DESI Data System (DS) is envisaged to encompass a QuickLook pipeline (QL) to precede the processing of raw spectrograph data that occurs off-site.  The DS is shown in Figure 1.  At the point where targets have been selected, fibers assigned and a next field selected, the DESI Online System (DOS) directs the telescope to take an exposure.  The raw data, and the fiber map that goes with it, are provided to 
the DS.  Additionally, information concerning the DESI GFA is available.  The data will be simultaneously routed to the offline spectroscopic pipeline, as well as to the QL.  The QL is expected to encompass a subset of the operations performed by the full Spectroscopic Pipeline to permit an online data quality assessment (QA).  The output will also provide feedback on the prediction of observed fluxes from the DESI GFA.  This document seeks to provide a description of the requirements for QL to facilitate its conceptual design.
1.2 DOS and QL Framework
Since the QL will run in the online environment, the DOS team will provide a software framework to run the Quick Look pipeline.  This framework will be based on analogous software from DES. A customizable user interface will permit modifications based on the QL needs.  Code is factored such that QL algorithms and pipeline components are developed independent of the framework and are implemented as executables in the software/products areas of the DOS eUPS.  The QL code, as run on the mountain, must be part of the DOS eUPS system.  The executables, input files and configuration parameters are wrapped as python components.  Each of these uses a particular file format, such as JSON or YAML for input configuration or FITS for certain inputs and outputs.  The framework provides access to these files, calls the executables, and writes the output XML which is then rendered as html pages with plots, tables and text.  DOS will provide the interface for displaying these data to the observers, but it will not use these data (or even require them to exist) for any automated decisions.  This allows DOS to continue with observations without a tight coupling to QL.
2. Metrics Requirements
The QL pipeline’s primary task is to document and ensure the proper operation of the spectrographs during operations.  This includes the provision of information that the DESI observers or experts may require to optimize ongoing observations.  In this section, we describe some of the metrics that will be probed by QL.  The offline pipeline has several steps, each of which are appropriate source points for QA metrics.  Raw images begin in a preprocessing step which applies dark subtraction, amplifier gains, bad pixel masks and pixel-level flat corrections.  The spectral extraction utilizes arc and continuum lamps, and fiber PSFs to obtain spectra.  A fiber flattening step is then followed by sky subtraction.  The flux calibration step may not be needed for the QL mission.  Further steps in the offline pipeline, such as redshift and classification determination are not considered necessary for QL goals.
2.1 Noise and bias measurements
	Noise assessment and bias measurement are required at all times to protect against a variety non-optimal conditions, such as temperature variations, unusual electronics noise or failure.  The CCD overscan region provides the means to test this performance for every exposure.  It will be necessary to monitor both the bias level and noise level for all CCDs, as a function of time.  It is worth noting that IMageBuilder in the DOS will also look at pixel-level noise diagnostics.  However, QL will look at these with an emphasis on their place in the spectroscopic pipeline, ie. How they affect observed spectra.  For instance, QL will need this information for further QA determinations later in the pipeline, such as when looking for sky above noise or calculating S/N.  QL is also required to look for the stability of these metrics, and to facilitate the quick identification of localized anomalous behavior.

2.2 Use of Sky in QA
Proper functioning of the spectrographs also requires that we establish that the projected flux is observed in the CCDs and that its response with wavelength is uniform and according to specifications. Nearly every fiber should have a detectable sky signature underlying the galaxy or standard star spectrum.  They will not have uniform flux levels, but current DESI simulations indicate the flux level should be statistically significant above noise in most if not all wavelengths.  At most wavelengths the sky spectrum will contribute the dominant flux.  The QL will compare the counts along the peak column of a given spectrum to the counts predicted from the overscan region on an image-by-image basis.
2.3 Standard stars
	It is unlikely that many of the survey galaxies will be bright enough to examine individually to establish a more detailed performance, so standard stars are critical.  The first priority is to extract counts vs. wavelength, which would permit a comparison to the ETC projection.  The next highest priority will be to flux calibrate these objects.  Current tests of the DESI offline flux calibration software indicate that this algorithm is fast enough for online use without modification.  The QL will provide output for each quadrant of the CCD in case one amplifier is lost.  These results should be sufficient to comment on ETC predictions and S/N vs. magnitude.
2.4 Galaxies
While the standard stars should provide ample flux at many wavelengths, they will only occupy a small fraction of fibers per exposure.  Galaxies will, of course, be present in almost all other fibers.  Additionally, we intend to produce a near real-time test of the efficacy of the data to support the primary BAO science by extracting sky-substracted galaxy spectra for the brightest galaxies in each exposure.  On the other hand, we are unlikely to perform redshift measurement and classification of all galaxies of the Survey.  The code will likely be too slow to benefit operations, and most galaxies will be too dim for a useful fast calculation with a potentially imperfect sky subtraction.  The clearest applicability of galaxies is to consider the brightest features to populate the CCD with photons more rapidly than the standard stars can achieve.  Over time, an integral flux can provide coverage of the whole CCD and supplement the standard star results by providing improved granularity on a more rapid timescale.  This might also involve the analysis of the brightest subsample, regardless of whether running in dark or bright time, of galaxies to complement the standard stars.  This would require information from the Targetting indicating the photometric brightness of the galaxy in each fiber.
2.5 Flux-based Data Quality Metrics:
The primary duty of the QL will be to establish the appropriate flux levels of the exposures.  Such measurements will occur at each of the stages of the QL pipeline.  It is anticipated that these determinations will require negligible resources compared to those needed for the offline pipeline, so in the interest of code reuse, these metrics are expected to be implemented in the offline pipeline.  This facilitates QA with the offline algorithms also.  Since these algorithms will be an important starting point for the QL algorithms, this will satisfy QL needs as well.

2.6  Warnings and Alarms
	An adjunct to the QL will be needed to operate on the QL outputs to apply thresholds or fits to confirm the spectroscopic data conforms to specifications, or to signal an error condition.  Since QL will have no link to automatically impact operations, these results will be informational for Observers or Experts.  They will permit a higher level summary and visualization of the information from the spectroscopic data.  With this in mind, and based in part on BOSS experience, we anticipate two different levels of message to be sent to the Observer.  Warnings will identify those areas where metrics are significantly non-optimal, but not prohibitive of doing science with the data.  For example, a worsening PSF will be flagged with a Warning if it is not dramatically non-optimal.  Alarms will be provided for those cases where metrics show a serious problem that jeopardizes science or might indicate a hardware problem.  The former might include fibers all consistent with sky and no targets, suggesting a pervasive positioning malfunction.  The latter might be evidence for shifted rows from the CCD, suggesting an electronics failure.  It will be up to the expert observer to decide how to respond to these messages.
3. Performance Requirements
3.1 Prioritization of outputs
Different outputs are processed with naturally different timescales, and with different levels of priority.  The fastest results will be available from the noise/bias analysis.  These results are also a very high priority to confirm spectrograph performance.  The analysis of standard stars will require a longer time scale, but is also a very high priority.  Lastly, the bulk analysis of galaxies is the most difficult step and is anticipated currently to have a relatively lower priority.  
3.2 Levels of operations capability
The use of the QL output is expected to have more than one context.  Shift-takers responsible for operating the telescope may not be experts on the online system or the telescope hardware.  These individuals require clear, concise and straightforward results that allow for unambiguous determination that projected system performance is being achieved.  Experts may also be present which require more detailed information and they may possess the ability to act on that information to rectify problems in system operations.  We can also envision a third type of individual who may be a shifter with specific skill set, but not at the most expert level.  The QL must be able to provide the outputs and functionality that each of these three categories of users require.
3.3 Requirements for Standard Stars and Galaxies
	In order to extract the observed counts vs. wavelength for QA, it will be necessary to obtain and process information for the sky and for the psf.  At the current time, prevailing judgment suggests that the spectra should be processed in a 1-dimensional analysis rather than the full 2-D analysis offline.  This will expedite the processing speed and should guarantee the most prompt results.  The QL needs to provide real-time assessment of spectrograph performance.  But some outputs may not require every image.  We will want to archive all outputs for later use, in case data-taking is affected.
4. Implementation Requirements
4.1 Computing resources
It is anticipated that QL will require at least 1 CPU core per CCD.  These 30 cores will have to handle all I/O, and processing described in Sections 2-4 of this document.  This might be one 32 core machine by the time we run in 2019.  This requirement is analogous to what BOSS uses for their QL.
4.2 Levels of output
The QL pipeline will be designed to provide maximum flexibility to the online system, so DOS has no dependencies on QL or its outputs.  On the other hand, it will be useful for DOS to have access to these outputs, and this access will be necessary to view the results on the mountain in the online environment.  This relationship is built into the structure shown in Figure 1, where the QL does not push data or communications to the DOS, but rather provides the information and the DOS can utilize it as appropriate.  However, there may be a need for sophisticated outputs for expert operators or other scientists.  There will also be different needs in commissioning, early data-taking, and long-term running.  At all periods, there is a potential need for fuller output for intermittent problem solving while the telescope is running.  To satisfy these diverse conditions, we consider that there will be three tiers of output from the QL.  An Observer level will provide the most straightforward quantities that all observers should understand and which give the most complete synopsis of the ongoing data quality.  An Expert level will provide the most detailed information and be accessible to experts in commissioning or problem-solving situations.  The QL should provide the means to switch between these modes with appropriate permission.  We consider a third Intermediate tier that may be used more during commissioning when observers will tend to have specific expertise relevant to ongoing operations.  This mode will provide a thorough set of outputs, but not to the detail required by all experts.   These tiers are indicated by initials in Table 1 thru Table 4.  For normal stable operations with a typical observer, the more detailed outputs will be turned off by default.  However, they will be available on demand, provided the appropriate permissions can be provided by the expert.
4.3 Required Inputs
The QL requires the same inputs as the offline spectroscopic pipeline.  These include raw spectrograph images, with overscan retained, to be available for every exposure.  This enables the dark/bias assessment, as well as any prompt determination of observed flux in the CCD.  The appropriate sky for standard star fibers, and possibly bright galaxy fibers, will be obtained from the online databases archived by DOS.   A target list will be required for each exposure to identify those spectra from standard stars and from survey galaxies.  The outputs from the Exposure Time Calculator (ETC) will also be needed, specifically the predicted S/N, sky background and flux counts vs. magnitude.  These parameters will be provided via flat files that are accessible by a query to the DOS. 4.4 Operating Modes
	As discussed above, there will be more than one operating mode for the QL, dictated by the promptness that some outputs can be ascertained.  The dark/bias outputs, for instance, can be calculated quickly, whereas counts vs. wavelength will take considerably longer.  There may be intermediate timescales for other outputs as we continue to specify the QL outputs.  For this reason, it is important that QL simultaneously process at each level of the pipeline independently.  To allow for this, multiprocessing capability will be needed so that QL can continue processing the more resource intensive tasks on one image until completion, while starting a new thread that promptly analyzes the newest image.

4.5 Interfaces Required
Several interfaces are involved in QL communicating with the online system to either receive information, or deliver it.  For those inputs that comprise files needed in the spectroscopic reduction, such as raw images, darks, pixel-level flats and arcs, we anticipate the DOS will facilitate a query interface for QL to identify the file location and name.  QL will not initiate any action by the DOS or camera readout, but will just look on disk, or in a database, for the inputs needed. For other system diagnostic information, particularly the predicted flux and S/N from the Exposure Time Calculator (ETC), this information will be provided by an online database.  The database will also need to provide information about targets on each fiber.  The outputs from QL will range from tables, to distributions, and will need to be databased as well as displayed in the online system.  The inputs and their interfaces are summarized in Table 5.
4.6 Outputs from QL
	Outputs primarily involved the kinds of metrics described in Tables 1-4, and derived quantities or plots relying on them.  It is expected that these outputs will be both archived, and submitted for display to observers on the mountain, as well as remote observers.  The archived data will be stored in a PostGres database, possibly independent of OpsDB.  In addition to the output results, the checks applied to them to establish data quality assurance will also be archived.   The archived data and checks will be in FITS or JSON format and will reside on the mountain.
	QL will provide results for display and archiving via interfaces specified in the online framework.  In addition, the framework will provide a means by which algorithm and pipeline developers can promptly update or modify specific display specifications for individual QL outputs or plots.  QL will ping the framework to initiate the process of sending the outputs to the display and archive.
	Two complexities arise in the requirements of the QL pipeline.  In one case, the determination of the interline sky continuum is required to be provided to the ETC to provide feedback.  This requires that this information not only be archived in the database, but that the ETC be notified of its existence.  In addition, DESI will operate by taking two exposures per field, which will need to be linked for at least some of the QL metrics.  Since the online framework is anticipated to invoke QL once per exposure, it will be necessary to provide the interfaces to information to keep track of preceding exposures of the same field.
4.7 Online and Offline Operation
	The QL pipeline as a whole will be encompassed in an executable that will be initiated online by the online framework as each exposure becomes available.  It will interact with the framework via interfaces that provide the needed inputs as well as the means to display and archive the outputs.  Internally, it will manage which of the pipeline algorithms are required to operate and it will ensure the presence of the necessary QL outputs or provide diagnostic information to the framework in case of error.  This approach simplifies the design of QL, and obviates the need for ICS to know which QL operations to spawn for a given exposure.  It is envisioned that QL will access a copy of the data, thereby not interacting with the actual data transfer handoff on the mountain.
	It is also anticipated that there will be a need to run QL offline, off of the mountain, asynchronously with data-taking.  This will be necessary for code development and testing, and remote work without internet connection.  There will also be occasions when there is a need to reproduce QL outputs, or update it with improved assessments.  It may be for development purposes of the algorithms, many of which will be the same offline algorithms used in the regular spectroscopic pipeline.  Therefore, we envision a standalone offline instance of QL that is able to obtain all necessary inputs, and provide to a display the necessary outputs.  This standalone mode will include running on an isolated computer such as a laptop.  Some of the services that permit inputs and outputs to and from QL will be different in the offline and online context.  This requires the framework for QL to permit it to be provided all of the inputs as in the online system, but in the offline or standalone manner.  This ‘workbench’ mode will also provide a simple avenue for the display of outputs that allows standalone operation.

ABSTRACT53
The Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) survey is a spectroscopic survey of tens of millions54
of galaxies at 0 < z < 3.5 covering 14,000 sq. deg. of the sky. In its first 1.1 years of survey operations, it55
has observed more than 14 million galaxies and 4 million stars. We describe the processes that govern56
DESI’s observations of the 15,000 fields composing the survey. This includes the planning of each57
night’s observations in the afternoon; automatic selection of fields to observe during the night; real-time58
assessment of field completeness on the basis of observing conditions during each exposure; reduction,59
redshifting, and quality assurance of each field of targets in the morning following observation; and60
updates to the list of future targets to observe on the basis of these results. We also compare the61
performance of the survey with historical expectations and find good agreement. Simulations of the62
weather and of DESI observations using the real field-selection algorithm show good agreement with63
the actual observations. After accounting for major unplanned shutdowns, the dark time survey is64
progressing about 7% faster than forecast, which is good agreement given approximations made in the65
simulations.66

1. INTRODUCTION69
The Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI)70
began a five year survey to measure redshifts of tens71
of millions of galaxies and quasars on May 14, 2021.72
Galaxies and quasars are selected to cover 0 < z < 3.573
over 14,000 sq. deg. of the sky. The resulting redshifts74
will be used to measure the expansion history of the uni-75
verse and the growth of structure to better understand76
the nature of dark energy (DESI Collaboration et al.77
2016a).78
The DESI survey consists of three programs. The dark79
program targets luminous red galaxies, emission line80
galaxies, and quasars, and covers 0 .4 < z < 3.5 (Zhou81
et al. 2023; Raichoor et al. 2023; Chaussidon et al. 2023).82
Dark program fields are observed whenever conditions83
are good and represent 90% of DESI’s effective observing84
time. The bright program targets a magnitude-limited85
sample of bright galaxies with 0 < z < 0.4, as well as86
Milky Way stars, and is observed when conditions are87
not good enough to observe dark fields (Hahn et al. 2022;88
Cooper et al. 2022). The combination of the dark pro-89
gram and the bright program are called the “main sur-90
vey.” Finally, a backup program observes bright stars91
and is only observed when conditions are too poor to92
observe bright program fields.93
These programs consist of a number of “tiles,” which94
are the combination of a location on the sky and an as-95
signment of fibers to locations in the field. The aim of96
operations is to observe these fields as efficiently as possi-97
ble. Two strategic goals drive many of the choices made98
in the DESI operations. First, we intend to observe in a99
“depth-first” mode, where we observe a given part of the100
sky to completion and never return to it, rather than a101
“breadth-first” mode where observations are spread over102
the full footprint each year. Second, we aim to observe103
z > 2.1 quasars four times each to improve the signal-to-104
noise ratio in the Ly-α forest, which enters into the DESI105
spectral coverage for redshifts z > 2.1 (DESI Collabora-106
tion et al. 2016a). This choice means that no observa-107
tions may overlap a past observation until the z > 2.1108
quasars have been identified, placing pressure on the109
survey to rapidly and robustly deliver quasar redshifts.110
These two goals are in tension with one another—the111
depth-first goal means that we intend to make overlap-112
ping observations quickly to finish parts of the sky, while113
the goal of identifying z > 2.1 quasars means that we114
must complete analysis of observations before we can115
make overlapping observations.116
Reconciling these goals means bringing together a117
large number of different processes and analyses on a118
daily basis to execute the survey. We focus in this pa-119
per on the survey in the time frame from 2021–05–14,120
the first day of the main survey, to 2022–06–14, when121
the Contreras wildfire temporarily shut down the sur-122
vey. Figure 1 shows the area of sky observed by DESI123
in the dark and bright programs during this period.124
We describe the DESI instrument in §2, and elabo-125
rate on this broad survey strategy in §4. We then de-126
scribe the different observational and analysis processes127
that take place on a near-daily basis in order to en-128
able the survey strategy in §5. The “merged target129
list”, which plays a central role in tracking the cur-130
rent state of DESI observations, is described in §6.131
The DESI sky footprint is defined in §3. The deliv-132
ered seeing, transparency, sky brightness, and uptime133
over the first 1.1 years are described in §7. We de-134
tail simulations of the survey in §8 and compare them135
with the observed survey performance to date. Finally,136

2. THE DARK ENERGY SPECTROSCOPIC140
INSTRUMENT141
The Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument is a 5000-142
fiber multi-object spectrograph on the Mayall telescope143
at Kitt Peak. The instrument and survey were con-144
ceived, designed, and built over a roughly ten year pe-145
riod from 2010–2020 (Levi et al. 2013; DESI Collabora-146
tion et al. 2016b, 2022). DESI was designed to measure147
the expansion history of the universe using the three-148
dimensional clustering of galaxies and the Lyman-alpha149
forest over the course of a five-year survey (DESI Col-150
laboration et al. 2016a). The instrument collects light151
from astronomical sources with the 4-m Mayall primary152
mirror and focuses it through the new corrector onto153
a 3.2 ◦ diameter focal plane (Miller et al. 2023). 5000154
robotically actuated fibers fill this focal plane (Silber155
et al. 2023), piping light through fibers to an array of156
ten high throughput spectrographs with three channels157
each spanning the wavelength range 3600–9800 ˚A.158
The focal plane is divided into ten “petals,” nearly159
identical wedges of the focal plane. Each petal has 500160
positioners, connects to one spectrograph, and contains161
a guide-focus array imaging camera (GFA). Four of the162
petals’ GFAs are dedicated to determining the focus of163
the instrument and deliver out-of-focus images. The164
other six deliver in-focus images and are used for guid-165
ing, point spread function measurements, and through-166
put measurements. The petals are designed to function167
independently of one another, so that problems with one168
petal do not affect any other petals.169
The main survey will observe millions of stars and170
galaxies over the course of five years. Initial results from171
the survey validation program are now available (DESI172
Collaboration et al. 2023a,b). The primary targets are173
quasars (Y` eche et al. 2020; Chaussidon et al. 2023),174
emission line galaxies with 0.6 < z < 1.6 (Raichoor et al.175
2020, 2023), luminous red galaxies with 0 .4 < z < 1176
(Zhou et al. 2020, 2023), bright galaxies with z < 0.4177
(Ruiz-Macias et al. 2020; Hahn et al. 2022), and stars178
(Allende Prieto et al. 2020; Cooper et al. 2022). Target-179
ing catalogs (Myers et al. 2023) for these images were180
drawn mainly from Data Release 9 of the DESI Legacy181
Imaging Surveys (Dey et al. 2019), which included imag-182
ing from the Dark Energy Camera on the Blanco tele-183
scope (Flaugher et al. 2015), the 90prime imager on the184
Bok telescope (Williams et al. 2004; Zou et al. 2017),185
and the Mosaic3 imager on the Mayall telescope (Dey186
et al. 2016). Targeting catalogs also incorporated flux187
and astrometric measurements from Gaia, the Wide-188
field Infrared Survey Explorer, and the Siena Galaxy189
Atlas (Gaia Collaboration et al. 2016; Cutri et al. 2013;190
Meisner et al. 2018; Schlafly et al. 2019; Moustakas et191
al. 2023).192
Each night, DESI observes roughly twenty tiles con-193
taining ∼100,000 sources. By the following morning,194
the offline pipeline automatically calibrates the result-195
ing exposures, extracts the sources’ spectra, subtracts196
background light, and fits the redshifts of the targets197
(Guy et al. 2023; Bailey et al. 2023). The performance198
of the pipeline was confirmed via a collaboration-wide199
effort to visually inspect tens of thousands of spectra200
and their derived redshifts (Lan et al. 2023; Alexander201
et al. 2023).202
The DESI GFAs and sky monitor provide real-time203
information on the seeing, transparency, and sky bright-204
ness seen by the Mayall (DESI Collaboration et al. 2022;205
Tie et al. 2020). This allows the DESI system to tune206
the length of exposures to achieve target depths; DESI207
closes the shutter and reads out the exposure when we208
have achieved the target signal to noise ratio (Kirkby et209
al. 2023, §5.7). This process allows us to produce spec-210
tra of relatively homogeneous quality even in changing211
conditions.212

3.1. Adjustments to tile centers290
The simple footprint definition of §3 describes our ba-291
sic footprint selection strategy. Many tile centers are292
additionally adjusted to avoid bright stars.293
The wide field of view (3.2 ◦) of DESI means that294
bright stars cannot be completely avoided. However,295
bright stars are particularly damaging if they fall in a296
few special parts of the DESI focal plane.297
First, it is problematic if a very bright star falls on a298
GFA. These can make it challenging to guide the tele-299
scope. Worse, the filter on the GFA reflects light falling300
outside of the GFA bandpass. Light from the bright star301
then ends up adding to a large out-of-focus ghost image302
covering a substantial portion of the DESI focal plane.303
This is avoided by shifting the tile centers to move bright304
stars off of the GFA filters. For tiles where a star with305
Gaia magnitude G < 6 lands nears a GFA, we searched306
for the smallest shift in RA or Dec, in steps of 10 arc-307
seconds, that would put the star at least 25 arcseconds308
from a GFA.309
Second, data from a petal can be rendered useless if310
a fiber is placed directly on a bright star, saturating311
large parts of the detector. This is mostly avoided by312
re-positioning such fibers (which will never have valid313
main survey targets) away from bright stars. But in314
rare cases a non-functional fiber happens to land on a315
very bright object. We adjust tile centers in these cases.316
After finding bright stars that land near the current set317
of non-functional positioners for each tile, we search for318
a small offset (up to 15 arcseconds) of the tile centers319
in order to minimize the total star light reaching non-320
functional positioners.321
We periodically compute new offsets for tile centers to322
account for new or bumped non-functional positioners,323
but we do not do this on the fly when designing each324
tile.325
4. SURVEY STRATEGY326
The goal of DESI is to observe a large, homogeneous,327
reproducible, and cosmologically interesting set of tar-328
gets over 14,000 sq. deg. of the sky (DESI Collaboration329
et al. 2016a). The survey further aims to operate in330
a “depth-first” fashion where all DESI observations in331
a particular region are completed before moving on to332
other parts of the sky.333
A critical constraint on the DESI survey strategy is334
that each DESI observation of a field depends on all335
earlier, overlapping observations of that field. This is336
primarily motivated by the need to identify z > 2.1337
quasars in fields from their initial observations, so that338
these Ly-α forest tracers can be targeted for repeat ob-339
servations on subsequent overlapping fields (DESI Col-340
laboration et al. 2016a). A secondary motivation is to341
obtain observations of targets where initial observations342
failed due to temporary glitches in fiber positioning or343
in the spectrographs. This dependence places impor-344
tant constraints on the survey strategy—an observation345
of a field cannot be made until earlier observations of346
all overlapping fields have been analyzed. In particular,347
no two overlapping fields of either the dark program or348
the bright program may be observed over the course of349
a single night.350
The DESI survey definition (DESI Collaboration et al.351
2016a) provides the basic information about each pro-352
gram, including the targets in each program, the amount353

The dark program is observed whenever conditions are369

motivated by placing the brightest targets in the worst376
conditions, so that systematic uncertainties are limited.377
As an added benefit, this approach reduces overheads378
by placing the exposures needing the shortest effective379

The next broad strategic element of the survey is to381
observe “depth-first”, completing all DESI observations382
of a particular region of the sky as soon as possible.383
This allows these regions of the sky to be available early384
for cosmological investigations, and allows many scien-385

a limited area). It also minimizes the negative impact387
of falling behind schedule; we would prefer to end the388
survey with a complete 13,000 sq. deg. survey than an389
inhomogeneous 14,000 sq. deg. survey. The depth-first390
goal is implemented in the nightly field selection ( §5.5)391
by preferring tiles near the celestial equator 1, tiles for392
which neighboring observations have been made, and393
tiles which have already been started but for which ob-394
servations are not yet complete.395

4.1. Airmass Optimization403
Survey planning assigns each tile an optimal hour an-404
gle. These optimal hour angles need to satisfy two re-405
quirements:406
1. The distribution of local sidereal time (LST)407
needed to observe all the tiles should match the408
distribution of local sidereal time expected to be409
available to the survey.410
2. The total time needed to finish the survey should411
be as short as possible.412
Alternatively, for the dark program, these requirements413
could be rephrased as asking how to minimize the air-414
mass of the observations subject to the time available to415
the survey.416
The airmass optimization algorithm for DESI is sim-417
ple. An initial guess of the assignment of hour angles to418
tiles is made by matching the LST distribution available419
to the survey to the right-ascension distribution of the420
survey’s tiles, weighted by the tiles’ expected observa-421
tion times. The initial assignments of tiles to LSTs is422
then further optimized through a simulated annealing423
process to minimize the total amount of time needed to424
observe the tiles, while maintaining the match between425

4.2. Slew Optimization463
Long slews reduce the amount of time each night dur-464
ing which DESI can be making science observations. A465
number of operations occur when ending one observa-466
tion and starting a new one (DESI Collaboration et al.467
2022):468
1. Spectrograph readout469
2. “Blind” positioner move470
3. Slewing & settling471
4. Field acquisition & guiding472
5. “Correction” positioner move473
The spectrograph readout and blind positioner move can474
occur simultaneously with slewing and settling, but the475
field acquisition and correction move must occur after476
slewing is complete. If the slew and settle time ex-477
ceeds ten seconds, slews begin to increase the overhead478
between exposures. Settling time is 8 seconds, and it479
takes 16 seconds to slew between adjacent DESI fields.480
So slewing adds to DESI overheads regardless of slew481
length.482
Nevertheless, even without any explicit slew optimiza-483
tion, slewing would only account for 3.1% of the open484
shutter time for the DESI survey, according to survey485
simulations. To try to reduce this, we do a simple greedy486
slew optimization where tiles nearby the current location487
of the telescope are preferentially observed. We penalize488
slews in the declination or negative right ascension direc-489
tions, but not in the positive right ascension direction,490
since we do not want to penalize slews that are trying to491
keep up with the sky rotation. This simple prescription492
reduces the slew time to 2.9% in simulations, and in-493
spection of the resulting slew patterns suggests limited494
potential for further improvement.495
5. SURVEY OPERATIONS496
Survey operations broadly refers to the process by497
which we complete the tiles composing the DESI dark,498
bright, and backup programs. Because past exposures499
inform future exposures, we cannot observe tiles over-500
lapping previously observed “pending” tiles until the501
analysis of those tiles has completed 2. So the basic op-502
erational scheme becomes:503
1. Each night, observe tiles that do not overlap the504
footprint of pending tiles.505
2. Each day, analyze observations and incorporate re-506
sults into the targeting ledger (merged target list507
or MTL; see §6), clearing pending tiles.508
If data reductions are delayed, we may skip step (2),509
in which case the footprint of pending tiles grows. We510
repeat this process until the survey is complete. The511
rest of this section details our implementation of this512
scheme.513
The ability to reproduce the particular set of targets514
that DESI ultimately observes is a key requirement of515
this process. We need to be able to simulate the ob-516
servational process on mock target catalogs in order to517
account for the effect of the DESI design on the final518
galaxy redshift catalogs. Accordingly, we must be capa-519
ble of reproducing the assignment of every fiber to every520
target over the course of the survey. Since these choices521
depend on the current observational state of the targets522
and the current health of the instrument, we need to523
track these quantities through time (see §6, §5.13). We524
record the state of both the targets and the instrument525
in ledgers. In these ledgers, each row is time-stamped526
and changes are made by appending new rows to the527
ledger indicating the new state of a target or fiber. Thus,528
past decisions about the assignment of fibers to targets529
can be reproduced by reading the ledgers through to the530
time at which those decisions were made.531

5.2. Effective Time595
The concept of “effective time” is important to DESI596
operations. We describe effective time briefly here; see597
Guy et al. (2023) for more details. Ultimately DESI598
seeks to measure the fluxes from distant galaxies to a599
specified accuracy. Rather than phrasing this accuracy600
in terms of the flux uncertainty at a particular wave-601
length, we parameterize it in terms of the amount of602
time it would take to reach a goal uncertainty in “nom-603
inal” conditions, defined to be 1.1 ′′ seeing, a sky back-604
ground of 21.07 mag per square arcsecond in the r band,605
photometric conditions, observations at zenith, through606
zero Galactic dust reddening. This “goal uncertainty” is607
weighted over wavelengths and spectral features in order608
to make it a good proxy for DESI’s ability to find a red-609
shift for a galaxy spectrum. Observations in the dark610
program aim for 1000 s of effective time, while bright611
program observations aim for 180 s.612

cated by the following effects:614
1. Poisson noise from source flux,615

large galaxies), and617

throughput.619

Tile design takes roughly thirty seconds. Two minutes801

large scale structure analysis of the final redshift cata-805
log, fiberassign inputs are all in the form of ledgers806
recording the state of the system and targets at any807
given time. Moreover, the complete state of the soft-808

On-the-fly assignment is convenient because it allows814

this mode, fiberassign does not know about future ob-820
servations and cannot adjust its assignment of fibers to821
targets using that information.822
Fiber assignment also needs access to the current state823
of the DESI focal plane. A substantial number of DESI824
positioners ( ∼ 700) cannot be assigned to science tar-825

Our approach to assigning LSTs to tiles starts with an initial guess. This initial guess is then optimized by a1872
simulated annealing algorithm, which perturbs the assignment to try to reduce the cost C.1873
To create the initial hour angle assignments, we first construct the cumulative distribution function of the tiles’1874
observational costs as a function of right ascension, CDF O(α). To construct this, we need to know what the ob-1875
servational cost of a tile is, and for that we need the tile’s airmass—but we do not know the tile’s airmass because1876
we have not yet assigned it an hour angle. For this initial guess we presume that all tiles will be observed with an1877
hour angle of zero. We also construct the cumulative distribution function of the available LST, CDF L(L), choosing1878
CDF L(Lstart) = 0 and integrating around the circle. We then find for each right ascension αi the corresponding LST1879
Li such that CDF O(αi) = CDF L(Li). Conceptually, this corresponds to matching the first 10% of the tiles in right1880
ascension to the first 10% of the LSTs (starting from Lstart), and so on, until all tiles have been mapped to LSTs. This1881
gives a mapping of tiles to LST that provides the initial guess for the simulated annealing. The only free parameter1882
in this initial guess is Lstart, the LST at which to start the cumulative distribution function; this corresponds to the1883
LST to which to map tiles with α = 0◦. We choose a number of Lstart values around the unit circle and use the Lstart1884
with the best score to produce the initial guess.1885
The simulated annealing process consists of a number of steps. In each step, we start by identifying LSTs where1886
changing the assignment of LSTs to tiles by one bin in LST would most significantly improve R, the component of1887
the cost coming from the difference between the planned and available times. These bins are identified by finding the1888
